# How I built the Lebanon Research Validation Workspace

[Concise timeline](TIMELINE.md) · [How I worked with AI](PROJECT_WORKFLOW.md) · [Curated source snapshots](CURATED_SNAPSHOTS.md) · [Security and verification](SECURITY.md)

## September 11, 2026 | The assignment that started it

Our lab was researching attacks on medical infrastructure in Southern Lebanon for Human Rights Watch. The assignment asked about attacks from March 2 onward. The part I brought to ChatGPT was concrete: find as many cases as possible where an attack on or near a hospital caused it to close, then establish how long services were interrupted, how many people were affected where we could tell, and what sources supported it. A report of a nearby strike was only the start of that investigation.

There were 14 of us in the lab, and our manual searches were hard to coordinate. I asked for “an open source research framework for high volume, high quality research.” Then I asked whether AI could automate some of it and added, “Obviously I would vet any findings myself.” I had very little coding experience and was paying for the AI tools myself. I went from asking how the system could work to “Build this for me.” Those three words is what set in motion a project that I consider to be the single greatest undertaking of my entire life. I still cannot believe how far this project has taken me.

The first thing I got back that afternoon was a workbook template and Apps Script for collecting leads, looking at sources, and sending potential findings to a human review queue. It gave me something I could inspect. The package and script passed static checks. I have no record of it handling a real Form submission or running live in the lab.

## September 12–13 | I chose a wider search and gave the Guide a separate job

The next thing I can document is a facility-first pilot. The early agent generated searches from English and Arabic names and geographic aliases, and the saved record distinguished a planned search from one it had run. I asked it to separate publication dates from event dates, check whether its initial screening had thrown away useful sources, and say when it could not open a source. I wanted a search that came up empty to leave the question open.

My first Guide helped me decide what should count. Filtering the national facility workbook to records tagged as hospitals would have left just 36 records in the provisional area. I was worried that a medical center could be tagged differently and disappear before anyone looked at it. We kept all medical facility categories in South and El Nabatieh, preserving the source's labels, and I approved 122 rows out of the national workbook's 1,383. Those rows gave us somewhere to start. They could include duplicate or unresolved sites and did not tell us how many incidents had happened.

I worked through 30 methodology questions before asking for a small demonstration. We would start from facility names and also work backward from reports of closures. Closure was the main research question. We would keep evidence of other disruptions, distinguish evacuation from closure, and record stages of reopening when the evidence supported them. As the early campaign grew, reporting also pointed to facilities outside the original workbook. The later planning register included 42 supplemental records alongside the 122 scoped rows.

I considered editing an earlier Masterbuild message or starting over once the method was clearer. Instead I kept the useful work, identified what needed to change, and preserved old results so they could be audited. If an earlier search had turned up a useful source, we could go back to that source and assess it under the current rules. We could not simply carry over the old conclusion. I put implementation and strategic review in separate projects and gave them a written state file so they could work from the same decisions. I even asked, “Quick question, should I have a guide for the guide?” I kept one active Guide and handed the next round of practical tests to Guide V2 and Master Build V2. Saying goodbye to the original Guide felt, in my words, “a tiny bit somber”, which in retrospect I find a bit funny.

I credit the way I organized the chats with making the whole project possible for me. The Guide helped me make sense of the work and write prompts. Masterbuild, and later Codex, carried out the implementation. I kept the context and decisions in files that a new Guide could pick up. [How I organized the project and handed work between chats](PROJECT_WORKFLOW.md)

## September 14–15 | A workbook I could demonstrate, and a release I refused to use

I wanted the person reviewing a finding to be able to see what the agent had searched, read the relevant source, and record their own assessment. The early workbook moved from rows of results to clickable case views, evidence links and review controls. I tried it myself and told the Guide, “I acted as a reviewer and it worked very well.” I also found that a source-intake test kit showed the original proof-of-concept outputs even after I submitted a new item. We had to distinguish a fresh submission and a fresh result from a bundled example.

I showed the prototype informally to the faculty lead and a lab leader. The faculty lead encouraged me to keep going and asked to see how the agent had searched, as I reported to the Guide that day. I took seriously the concern that a complicated workbook could intimidate someone opening it for the first time. I wanted the agent to surface leads, source gaps and suggested searches, then leave people a clear way to do the additional work themselves.

The next workbook release tried to do too much in one pass. I opened it in Excel and the navigation and source links were broken. The forensic report attributed the problem to a workbook rewrite that damaged hundreds of hyperlink caches. A recovery candidate passed its automated checks and still gave me trouble in Excel. I kept the exact workbook I had personally tested and put the more ambitious governance changes on a separate development track. After that experience, I cared which exact file a person had tested in Excel before I called it ready.

## September 16–24 | I wanted researchers to investigate, not just check boxes

After another discussion with the faculty lead, I worried that my workbook made serious verification look like “one person basically checking boxes.” The cases could take days to understand. A researcher might need to find independent reporting, distinguish two episodes at one facility, add a source, or explain why one part of a claim could not be verified.

I asked for the actual research trail. That meant showing the proposition, the source passage, contradictions and gaps, and making it possible to reopen a conclusion. I asked the Guide to challenge the design for overengineering. We reduced it to a smaller shared data model. The audit trail could retain what the agent had searched, including rejected hits, while the reviewer saw the material that mattered to the finding. I also reconsidered what “done” meant. I wanted continued research and verification to stay at the center. Mapping and reporting could use the evidence we had at a given point.

The September 18 charter captured that change. A read-only web prototype showed how separate cases, claims and sources would fit together. By September 24 a runnable local web pilot survived. Excel still had uses, but it no longer had to carry concurrent editing, a shared evidentiary record and revision history on its own. The complete local pilot and the much smaller hosted development slice were different things.

## September 25–30 | The implementation process had become its own problem

On September 25 I pointed out something I had missed in the web design. A Supervisor needed a way to defer an incident or decide it should be left out. I also asked what stood between the development slice and a site the lab could use. I set October 2 as a target for a small evaluation with my professor, lab leader, and a few researchers. That was a goal for the pilot. We were still working through the steps that would make it possible.

I already had a researcher interface I largely wanted to keep. Getting from a small hosted development site to something the lab could use had turned into a long series of manual rehearsals. With ChatGPT as my Guide and ChatGPT Work as Masterbuild, I was the one running commands and tests on my computer. For about a week and a half I’d spend hours troubleshooting, trying another command, and running test after test. Some of the checks did tell us something. On September 29 a browser timing check failed, and on September 30 another attempt stopped when a browser function was unavailable. I felt like I was doing hours of work and getting almost nowhere. I described it at the time as “toying around with its rehearsals almost endlessly.”

## September 30 | The conversation with Kenneth that saved the project

I happened to meet Kenneth Bundy at a point when I was discouraged, angry that the work wasn’t moving, and skeptical that the Guide was guiding me in the right direction. I felt up against a wall. I needed something to show the faculty member in the lab, and I was burning out. That short conversation with Kenneth truly saved this project. Would I have figured it out? I think so, but who knows.

Kenneth told me to try Codex. Up until then I thought you needed to know how to code to use it. He also suggested putting the project in a GitHub repo and using two things called Next.js and Vercel. I’d never heard of either, and it sounded like gibberish to me at the time. After a couple of prompts describing his recommendations to the Guide, I was back off to the races. The work was moving at a steady pace again.

The first step was to gather the code, database schema, specifications, and research in one place so Codex could see what I had already done. I was worried the newest build chat only had enough context for the three-case pilot and would leave the earlier research behind. We went back through earlier builds, local exports, and handoffs to recover what belonged in the larger project.

## October 1–7 | I moved the build to Codex and asked for my workflow back

The first Next.js version proved it could authenticate users and save drafts with revision checks. It also left out much of the researcher interface and had a limited Supervisor Hub. I rejected the interface as a product, kept the engineering foundation, and asked Codex to restore the experience I had accepted. We compared paired previews. Codex restored the researcher and supervisor paths on top of the shared backend. I could then test sign-in, private drafts, adding evidence, reopening a case and exporting approved work.

I made a substantive change to what a Supervisor’s decision meant. A case could be useful with unanswered questions if a Supervisor made an explicit judgment and recorded the limitations. Unknown findings stayed unknown. Deferring a requirement did not make a fact true. Reopening a case could invalidate its current acceptance while keeping the earlier decision in the history.

I also challenged the migration numbers. I wanted to know where the *incidents* the engine had found had gone. A crosswalk of facility records did not answer that. During the rollout preparation I spotted a second problem. A converter had used reduced capacity as a fallback for unrelated source statements such as injuries, transfers or continued operation. The import could pass its tests and still misrepresent the research. I asked for a focused correction. The resulting review remapped some proposals and kept others as provenance without turning them into facts.

On October 6 I accepted the local product after using it myself. The hosted rollout had its own authorization and checks. Its execution receipt records the bounded import and hosted pilot. Slow loads then became a concrete performance problem. Codex measured browser and backend behavior separately and reduced unnecessary snapshot work. Weekly Research Analytics gained a Supervisor-only view for saved reports, with an explicit empty state for an uncaptured week.

## October 8–16 | I found holes in discovery and reconsidered where leads belonged

Straightforward searching had surfaced material I expected the original research engine to find. That made me question what we were measuring. A record could be missing from the website because it had never been found, because we had matched it to the wrong site or episode, or because the research had never made it through the import. I wanted those possibilities investigated before calling the research complete.

I asked for more systematic use of the Insecurity Insight series and the underlying dated records. I wanted to use the dates for focused searches, including searches for facilities we did not already know about. I also wanted promising leads to survive with their sources even if we could not yet identify a premises or admit an incident. The investigations exposed another counting problem. A lead could be a new facility, another episode at a known facility, another source for an existing episode, or a question we still could not resolve.

The lab had already gathered a little over a hundred news articles, as I remember it, and people were still sorting through them. I wanted that work to carry forward into the website. Starting with a blank system would have made the switch feel like a step backward to the researchers, the Lebanon project lead, and the lab’s faculty administrator. We went through the existing submissions and prepared evidence and leads that could be reviewed in the new workflow. The intake audit covered 93 spreadsheet submissions. A submission could point to several sources or repeat a source someone else had already found. Each proposed addition kept its link to the original submission and needed the same review as new evidence.

At first we tried a read-only Research Leads view. After talking with the lab leader, I wanted researchers to be able to build up a lead, reuse evidence, combine related material reversibly and ask a Supervisor to advance it when justified. I also wanted strong cases to avoid an unnecessary second intake. Codex prepared a hybrid approach locally. The figure of 242 referred to lead holders, and the hybrid package had not shipped when that Guide ended.

I later decided the collaborative workbook was the better place for this exploratory work at that stage. The website could concentrate on incidents and their evidence. That was an operating choice about where the lab would do each job. When the transition also removed useful improvements from the incident interface, I asked for those improvements to be restored. The lesson for me was that simplifying a product still requires knowing exactly what you mean to preserve.

## October 16–30 | The lab had to be able to use it on its own

Once the website was going into regular use, a researcher’s first sign-in and their next click became as important as a successful build. Onboarding exposed the difference between a working login and an account that could actually open its workspace. I worked through where an assigned case appeared, what a researcher saw beside a finding, and what a Supervisor could do with an unresolved case. The incident layout evolved toward visible next actions, clearer evidence roles and less scrolling, while keeping the exact saved passage available.

Drawing on my GIS minor, I wanted the reviewed evidence to travel into ArcGIS without manually rebuilding the dataset. I directed coordinate review and two export shapes for different mapping tasks. The export needed to preserve which physical facility the point referred to, how we knew, and which values were uncertain. A private ArcGIS Online trial on synthetic data saved and reopened maps and checked the source CSV bytes. It did not establish complete value and geometry readback from every reopened layer. I kept that limitation separate from the parts that worked.

By October 30 the lab needed a usable incident interface. I kept that release compatible with the existing database and left more ambitious geography changes for separate verification. Then the obvious problems were the ones users ran into. Add Evidence could fail without an explanation because a required note was hidden inside a collapsed section. A researcher could reopen a draft and still have no way to edit the staged changes. Codex fixed both. I reopened a draft, edited it, saved it, and refreshed to check that the change persisted. Later autosave work made the save state clearer. Text sitting in an unfinished form still needed a durable save.

## November 3–7 | Real lab feedback changed the rules and the screens

With the lab working in the shared application, I also needed a way to handle account problems. I chose a narrow owner-admin workflow for existing accounts with an authenticator challenge. That kept administrative access separate from routine research.

I wanted a researcher who spotted a problem in a shared fact to be able to explain the correction and attach the evidence. Asking a Supervisor to reconstruct the whole case from a flag meant asking them to do the researcher’s work again. We changed the flow so a researcher could submit a correction package and a separate Supervisor could evaluate it with reasons and history. I also asked for reviewers to explicitly select the evidence they relied on for factual approval. Ordinary authorized fact validation could be completed by a researcher. Independently reviewing a correction and making a case-level Supervisor decision remained distinct responsibilities.

I explored visual prototypes and initially declined both broad redesigns. Later I liked the Spartan Research Studio treatment enough to keep working on it in a separate preview. I wanted the interface to respond visibly when someone opened a source, selected evidence, or saved a draft, while preserving the layout people already knew. Before moving the accepted version live, a mixed-version check showed that an open tab could request an old bundle and get a 404. We had the lab save its work and pause before the cutover. The live check afterward confirmed that the researcher workspace, saved draft, evidence and Supervisor queue opened.

Then a researcher showed me a review she could save but could not submit. I tried it myself and saw the same rejection. The explanation required for conflicting or qualifying evidence lived in a different field from the reviewer notes. I wanted the form to catch that earlier and let me edit the staged review straight from **Review Changes**. I also wanted a genuinely completed Supported fact card to turn green and keep its text status, so I could see what remained at a glance. A private draft or pending submission was too early to show completion. The same release work added multiple assignments and private notes, and fixed an assignment dialog that could disappear during navigation. A separate fact-resolution report stayed open because we had not reproduced it.

## November 6–11 | I wanted to know whether the second engine could actually find incidents

I wanted to learn how agents worked well enough to build or change them myself. The second engine assigned investigators and evidence reviewers different jobs, kept source receipts, and could resume a bounded investigation from a checkpoint. I asked for flexible date-first and facility-first inputs, coverage of alternate names, and controlled comparisons that would show whether a new search method actually helped. I also wanted formal facts to match the categories the lab’s workspace was designed to collect.

The benchmarks gave me evidence I could disagree with. In one known-case run, the engine recovered 19 of 25 positive episodes exactly, with five partials and one miss. Improvements to retrieving and presenting source passages could help handoff without helping incident discovery. One worker received a truncated evidence packet even though its saved capture was intact. That drove a check at the delivery boundary and a recovery path from committed receipts. Another experiment reserved one source open for the affected building. It added no exact recoveries in the matched comparison, so we kept the existing method.

On November 10, in a supervised wave using Ukraine education cases, ten investigators and ten separate reviewers found all nine reference leads and recovered eight episodes exactly. One remained partial and both date-first controls stayed held. That told me something about the test we had actually run. The Lebanon evaluation still needed its own evidence and authorization. The research engine remained a separate development project from the website the lab was using.

In November I asked for another handoff before a Guide ran out of room. I also questioned whether a successor Guide should be making code changes when I’d only asked it about the UI. I wanted the freedom to talk through an idea and decide when it was ready to build.

## November 11–12 | I wanted the final reviewer to be able to see and reproduce the work

The incident page still needed a clearer entry point. I asked for **Review facts and evidence**, **Next research steps**, and the coordinate action to be easy to find. I wanted clickable help for first-time users, visible cues for required fields, and the exact saved excerpt alongside the finding it supported. An exact-commit defensive review preceded the application release. I checked the live workflow in an authorized session. The Analytics view could show an uncaptured week, which was a separate matter from saving a real report.

I wanted someone new to the project to be able to run it from scratch. I put together a separate portfolio with synthetic data and verified the setup on Windows, Ubuntu and macOS. In the operational project, a defensive assessment led to dependency and failure-handling fixes. I asked for the build checks to be rerun so the downloaded evidence could be matched to the source that had actually been tested.

When I read that first expanded history, I still recognized the features more easily than I recognized my own experience. I went back to the Guide conversations to recover the choices, setbacks and changes of direction that shaped the project. This timeline brings those back into the story.
